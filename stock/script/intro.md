America's constitutional system was built on a fundamental principle: **no person is above the law, including the President of the United States.**



The presidency carries extraordinary power. A president can influence the direction of the nation, shape domestic and foreign policy, command the executive branch, appoint judges and government officials, and affect the lives of millions of Americans. But the Constitution does not grant unlimited authority. It establishes a system of checks and balances designed to prevent the concentration, abuse, and misuse of power.



When presidential conduct raises serious questions about corruption, abuse of authority, obstruction of constitutional responsibilities, or betrayal of public trust, the American people have a right to understand what happened, what the evidence shows, and how their constitutional system can respond.



**This roadmap has a clear purpose: to examine the evidence of Donald Trump's conduct in office, evaluate that conduct against the Constitution's impeachment framework, and build a factual and constitutional case for his removal from office through the lawful process established by the Constitution.**



This is not an exercise in political disagreement. It is an examination of presidential power, constitutional limits, accountability, and the responsibility of government officials to uphold the law.

## The Difference Between Outrage and Accountability



Political anger can draw attention to misconduct. It cannot establish a constitutional case.



Any serious effort to pursue presidential removal must be grounded in credible evidence, careful analysis, and a process capable of withstanding public, political, and legal scrutiny.



This roadmap will examine difficult questions:

- What actually happened?
- What evidence confirms it?
- What did the President know?
- Did the President direct, approve, participate in, or knowingly facilitate the conduct?
- Was presidential authority used unlawfully, corruptly, or in a manner that seriously undermined constitutional responsibilities?
- Does the substantiated conduct constitute an impeachable offense?
- What constitutional grounds support the articles of impeachment that may be warranted?
- Can the evidence and conclusions withstand scrutiny from people who do not share the same political views?

These questions are not obstacles to accountability. They are the foundation of accountability.



The objective is not to manufacture accusations or exaggerate misconduct to achieve a political result. It is to establish the strongest factual and constitutional case the evidence supports—and to correct, reject, or acknowledge any claim that does not withstand examination.



A case built on verifiable evidence is stronger than one built on rhetoric. A constitutional argument that addresses opposing interpretations is more credible than one that ignores them. And a process that respects the law is more difficult to dismiss as political persecution.



## A Constitutional Process—Not a Shortcut



The Constitution provides a mechanism for addressing presidential misconduct through impeachment and, upon conviction in the Senate, removal from office. It does not guarantee that removal will occur, nor does it permit Congress to bypass its constitutional responsibilities.



The process has distinct stages, each with its own authority and threshold.



### The Path to Constitutional Removal



**STEP 1 — INVESTIGATE**



Establish the factual record. Obtain documents, testimony, financial records, official directives, and other relevant evidence through lawful investigative means.



**STEP 2 — EVALUATE**



Analyze the substantiated evidence and determine whether the conduct may constitute impeachable offenses under the Constitution.



**STEP 3 — HOUSE OF REPRESENTATIVES**



Present the findings and proposed articles of impeachment. The House debates and votes on impeachment.



**STEP 4 — SENATE TRIAL**



If the House impeaches, the Senate conducts the constitutional trial, considers the evidence and arguments, and votes on conviction.



**STEP 5 — CONVICTION AND REMOVAL**



If two-thirds of the senators present vote to convict, the President is removed from office.



Each stage matters. Investigations that lack credibility weaken the case. Articles of impeachment that overreach can undermine support. A House impeachment without a Senate conviction results in impeachment—but not removal.



Understanding the entire process is essential to pursuing a successful constitutional accountability effort.



## Why the Public Has a Role



The Constitution assigns impeachment responsibilities to Congress, but Congress does not operate in isolation from the people it represents.



Citizens have the right to question their elected officials, demand transparency, ask for investigations, examine the evidence, attend public meetings, communicate their concerns, and evaluate how their representatives respond.



Public participation does not replace congressional judgment. It is part of representative government.



This roadmap is intended to give citizens the knowledge to engage in that process intelligently—to understand what they are asking their representatives to do, why they are asking for it, and what evidence should justify each step.



Rather than relying on slogans, rumors, or social media outrage, citizens should be equipped to make specific, informed requests:

- Investigate credible allegations of presidential misconduct.
- Obtain relevant records and testimony through lawful congressional authority.
- Protect independent oversight and whistleblowers.
- Publish substantiated findings and explain unresolved allegations.
- Evaluate the constitutional significance of the evidence.
- Pursue articles of impeachment if the findings warrant them.
- Fulfill constitutional responsibilities regardless of party affiliation.

The responsibility of elected officials is to the Constitution and the country—not simply to their party, political allies, or the President.

## The Responsibility We Share



The removal of a president is one of the most consequential decisions the American constitutional system can make. It should never be taken lightly. But the constitutional tools of accountability should not be ignored, weakened, or abandoned when serious misconduct is supported by credible evidence.



This roadmap is not asking citizens to accept conclusions without examining the evidence. It is asking them to examine the evidence, understand the Constitution, and recognize what is at stake when presidential power is exercised.



We may disagree about politics. We may disagree about policy. We may even disagree about the conclusions drawn from particular evidence. But the principle that no president is above the Constitution cannot depend on partisan convenience.



If the evidence establishes presidential misconduct serious enough to warrant impeachment, Congress must not dismiss, conceal, or ignore that evidence for political convenience. The Constitution provides Congress with the authority and responsibility to investigate, consider impeachment, and hold a president accountable through the process it establishes.



The public, in turn, has the right to demand transparency, integrity, and action from its elected representatives.
